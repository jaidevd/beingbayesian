Being Bayesian

Stress-Testing India's Loudest Stories with Open Data and FOSS

Every day, social media, the news and even the government sell you loud stories. This talk is about the unglamorous discipline of checking them anyway — with public microdata, a Jupyter notebook, and the FOSS stack.

Jaidev Deshpande  ·  beingbayesian.in

Hi, I'm Jaidev. For the last two years I've written a newsletter called Being Bayesian, where I take apart popular, confidently-stated claims about the Indian economy and society using the country's own public microdata. Today I want to walk you through that process, four times, on four claims you've probably already heard this year.

You've heard these already this year

“Quick commerce is killing kirana stores.”

“Gig work is liberating India's poor and uneducated.”

“The only way to make it in this market is a 90-hour week.”

“Welfare is making people lazy — and wasteful.”

Confident. Specific. Loud. And almost never checked.

Digital transformation experts say quick commerce is killing kirana stores. Startup bros say gig work is liberating India's poor and uneducated. Captains of industry say the only way to make it is a 90-hour week. Influencers-turned-economists say welfare is making people lazy. Every one of these is stated with total confidence, and almost nobody checks it against anything.

The problem

“The amount of energy needed to refute bullshit is an order of magnitude bigger than that needed to produce it.”
— Alberto Brandolini, the Bullshit Asymmetry Principle

Fighting misinformation isn't just hard. It's Sisyphean.

The programmer Alberto Brandolini coined this: the energy needed to refute bullshit is an order of magnitude bigger than the energy needed to produce it. No one person wins against the flood of clickbait, virality and tiny attention spans. You're pushing a boulder up a hill that resets every day.

Being Sisyphus is the job — and FOSS is the leverage

As open-data and FOSS people, we're uniquely positioned to do this. India quietly publishes some of the richest microdata in the world — the National Sample Survey, PLFS, HCES, the Time Use Survey — and that's only a fraction of what's out there.

Armed with this data and a Jupyter notebook, we can dismantle even the loudest stories.

Jupyterpandasmatplotlibstatsmodelsgit

This talk isn't a statistics lecture. It's an argument that good-faith, reproducible, open-data analysis is civic infrastructure — and that exercising it is our civic duty. And it turns out the tools we already love — Jupyter, pandas, matplotlib, statsmodels, git — are the entire stack this job needs. Nothing proprietary, nothing exotic.

Case 1 · Retail

Quick commerce is killing kirana stores

“About 2,00,000 kirana stores have shut down in the last year because of predatory e-commerce practices.” AICPDF (traders' federation), widely reshared as breaking, shocking data

Gut check 200,000 sounds huge. Huge compared to what?

This one came from a trade federation and went everywhere on social media with SHOCKING DATA energy. Before opening a dataset: 200,000 is a big number in isolation. But India doesn't have a small number of kirana stores. Let's find out how big.

India has about 13 million kirana stores

200,000 closures against ~13M kirana stores1.5% national closure rate
… even limited to metro India alone5%
Households that shop online for groceries at all7% — 93% stay offline
… even inside urban India specifically83% still don't shop online
Historical base rate: Amazon/Flipkart-era, COVID lockdown surgedisplaced only 1–2%, then ~0.5%

HCES 2023–24

India has roughly thirteen million kirana stores. Two hundred thousand closures against that base is a 1.5% closure rate nationally, 5% if you only count metros. And only 7% of Indian households shop online for groceries at all — 93% never do. Even in urban India specifically, 83% of households don't shop online. This isn't new either: Amazon and Flipkart's big retail push only ever displaced 1 to 2% of local stores, and even the COVID lockdown e-commerce surge only got to about half a percent.

The real rival isn't Blinkit

E-commerce can reach at most 7% of Indian households — kirana's real rival is infrastructure, education and income inequality, not quick commerce.

Who actually shops online is basically a map of urban privilege: having piped cooking gas at home makes a household 4× more likely to be an online grocery shopper; going from a graduate to a postgraduate degree doubles the odds. The closures may be real — the cause on offer wildly overstates its reach.

So the posterior here isn't "the closures didn't happen" — kirana stores do close, for lots of reasons. It's that the specific mechanism being blamed can, structurally, only ever touch 7% of households. And that 7% isn't randomly distributed — it's piped gas, postgraduate degrees, urban Tier-1 addresses. The story blames a phone app for what's actually an infrastructure and inequality problem.
Case 2 · Labour

Gig work liberates India's poor and uneducated

“Workers who once faced barriers like lack of education or technical skills now earn ₹15,000 to ₹45,000 or even more.” the platform-economy narrative, echoing NITI Aayog's own optimistic framing

Gut check Where do we even find “gig workers” in a labour survey?

This is the LinkedIn-influencer-as-amateur-economist claim: platforms have liberated people once locked out by lack of education, and here are the numbers to prove it. Before we even reach for PLFS: does the phrase "gig worker" mean anything to India's official labour classification?

PLFS has exactly four kinds of worker

PLFS categoriesself-employed · regular wage/salaried · casual labour · not in workforce
A “gig worker” boxdoes not exist — they scatter across all four
Apply NITI Aayog's own 2022 proxy filter (urban, age 18–45, bottom 75th pctile of spend)the ₹15,000 floor holds up — the ₹45,000 ceiling doesn't
Among men in that filtered populationno meaningful income gain from more education
PLFS sorts every worker into one of four buckets, and none of them is "gig worker." So I did the next best thing: took NITI Aayog's own 2022 proxy definition — urban, 18 to 45, bottom three-quarters of per-capita spend — and applied it to the actual PLFS microdata. The 15,000-rupee floor of the claim holds up fine. The 45,000 ceiling doesn't. And within that filtered population, men see no real income bump from more education — which quietly undercuts the "liberation" framing on its own terms.

You can't check a claim about a category that doesn't exist

You can't even count India's gig workers — the surveys have no box to put them in, so the “liberation” story can never be checked against the workers it claims to describe.

Sometimes the most honest — and most powerful — finding is that the data simply cannot answer the question. That itself should give anyone celebrating this liberation some pause.

This is the case where knowing when to stop matters as much as the investigation itself. I can't hand you a clean number for the gig economy's true reach or true pay, because the government's own instrument has no way to see it. That absence is the finding.
Case 3 · Work & Care

Just work 90 hours a week

N R Narayana Murthy — 70 hrs/week · S N Subrahmanyan — 90 hrs/week · Bhavish Aggarwal — 140 hrs/week. “work–life balance is a western concept”

Gut check What would 90 hours a week actually cost — in sleep, in family time?

Three captains of industry, three escalating numbers, and the explicit dismissal of work-life balance as a foreign import. So: if a salaried, white-collar Indian actually worked 90 hours a week, what would have to give?

The hours are already unequal

Salaried, white-collar paid workmen ~56 hrs/wk · women ~48.6 hrs/wk
Unpaid domestic choreswomen do 4× as much as men
Even in this salaried cohort, men's kitchen timebarely 1 hour a week — near-absent from serving/cleanup
Kitchen time by education & marriage (women)graduates ~12.5 hrs/wk, nearly doubling below graduate level; marriage alone adds ~10 hrs/wk

2024 Time Use Survey (ICATUS)

Start with the baseline: salaried men already work about 56 hours a week, women about 48.6 — only an hour a day less, despite the perception gap. But women do four times the unpaid domestic work men do. And even in this cohort of salaried, supposedly-emancipated households, men spend barely an hour a week cooking, and are almost entirely absent from serving food or cleaning up after.

What would 90 hours actually cost?

Best case: both genders cap chores at 1 hr/daywomen's ceiling rises to only ~67 hrs/wk
To reach 90, you'd have to cut~1 hr sleep/night, all TV (10–12 hrs/wk), most talk & text time (15 hrs/wk)
Deficit still remaining after those cutswomen 22 hrs · men 34 hrs
Even in the most generous scenario — everyone caps chores at an hour a day — women's work-time ceiling only rises to 67 hours a week. Getting anyone to 90 means cutting an hour of sleep every single night, giving up television entirely, giving up most talking and texting with people you care about. And you're still 22 hours short for women, 34 for men.

Same ask, wildly different bill

A 90-hour week doesn't just cost sleep and Netflix. Because women already carry 4× the unpaid work, the same “ask” costs men and women completely different things.

Asking what a 90-hour week would cost a salaried Indian — in sleep, in family time, in the unpaid care work women already shoulder — settles the argument far better than any motivational LinkedIn post.

This is the essay where the honest posterior isn't a single number — it's that "just work more" is not a gender-neutral instruction. The 90-hour week was proposed as a universal grind; the data says it's actually two very different asks wearing the same headline.
Case 4 · Welfare

Welfare makes people lazy — and wasteful

Poorest 40% of households spend 4% of their budget on paan/gutkha/tobacco vs. only 2.5% on education — intoxicant spend up 33% in a decade — set beside rising Ayushman Bharat (PMJAY) enrolment. Economic Advisory Council to the PM, op-ed using HCES data — the “moral hazard” framing

Gut check Are the households getting insurance the same ones spending more on gutkha?

Two of the Prime Minister's own economic advisors wrote this: government spending on health insurance is up, spending on intoxicants is up, therefore moral hazard — people take more risks because they feel insured. The hole is immediate: two things rising together doesn't mean one causes the other. Are we even looking at the same households?

Check the same households, not two trend lines

Urban PMJAY/scheme beneficiaries vs. non-beneficiariesspend ₹2/month more on gutkha
Rural beneficiaries vs. non-beneficiariesspend ₹4/month less
Entire spread across every group in the chart±₹15

HCES, propensity-score matched on household size, caste, religion & region — not a raw comparison

Instead of two national trend lines, I compared insured and uninsured households directly, split rural and urban. But not a naive comparison: households are first matched by propensity score on things like size, caste, religion and region, trimmed to common support, then compared within matched strata — the same rigor you'd want turned on any of the loud claims we started with. Urban beneficiaries spend about two rupees a month more on gutkha than urban non-beneficiaries. Rural beneficiaries actually spend four rupees less than rural non-beneficiaries. The entire chart, top to bottom, spans about fifteen rupees.

Not a scandal. A rounding error.

Free healthcare buys the average household two rupees more gutkha a month. Moral hazard, measured — and it isn't there.

This is the dreaded link between public healthcare and "wasteful" vice spending, and it comes down to a difference of about two rupees a month. That's the posterior: the claim was directionally testable, we tested it, and it evaporates.

But welfare's real effects hide where headlines don't look

LPG-subsidy households spend nearly ₹60 more on education per student — and two-thirds of that goes straight into private coaching.

While the headline claim about vice spending falls apart, a genuine welfare effect was sitting one tab over, on a subsidy nobody was even arguing about. An LPG subsidy meant for cooking gas quietly turns into a child's tuition fee. That's the kind of finding no headline thinks to go looking for.

Step back and the pattern repeats: a confident, specific, viral claim, and underneath it, once you actually pull the microdata, something smaller, stranger, or simply unanswerable. None of these took more than a public dataset and a notebook.

Meet the alphabet soup

HCES, PLFS, TUS — three names that kept coming back. They're not three different things.

SurveyWhat it measuresWhere it showed up
HCES
Household Consumption Expenditure Survey		what households spend, and on whatkirana stores · welfare & vice
PLFS
Periodic Labour Force Survey		who works, how, and for how muchgig economy

All three are run by the same apparatus — India's National Sample Survey system, under the Ministry of Statistics (MoSPI) — and all three are free to download.

Every one of these four case studies leaned on a government survey with an acronym, and it's worth pulling those acronyms apart, because they all come from the same place: MoSPI's National Sample Survey apparatus. HCES tells you what people spend money on. PLFS tells you who's working and how. TUS tells you what people actually do with their day. Different questions, same machinery.

They all share one shape

Household fileone row per household — location, assets, income bracket
→
Person fileone row per person — age, education, an occupation code
→
Item / activity fileone row per thing bought or minute logged

Joined by a household & person ID — and every code in every file means nothing without a separate codebook.

NIC industry code 62, in the TUS person file“Computer programming, consultancy and related activities”
HCES cereals item code 129, in the item filea hidden subtotal, not a real item — skip it and every food total silently doubles

The friction isn't the file format — it's usually a plain CSV. It's translation, and the codebook doesn't warn you about its own landmines.

Every one of these surveys ships as several linked files, not one tidy spreadsheet: a household file, a person file, sometimes an item or activity file, joined by ID columns. And every interesting column is a number that means nothing on its own. To find "salaried white-collar workers" for the 90-hour-week essay, I needed the National Industrial Classification codebook just to know that industry code 62 means computer programming. That's translation. But some codes are worse than untranslated — HCES sneaks a subtotal into the middle of every item list, coded like any other item. Forget to drop code 129 before summing the cereals columns and you've silently double-counted every household's grain spend. The format is never the problem. The government's own vocabulary is, and sometimes it's actively booby-trapped.

Pick the wrong unit, get the wrong story

Every file lets you collapse it to a household, a person, or an item — and each choice gives a different, equally “correct” number.

Kirana stores — collapsed to household7% of households shop online
Welfare & vice — collapsed to per adult₹2/month more, per adult — not per household
90-hour week — collapsed to person56 vs 48.6 hours, per person per week

Average the intoxicant spend per household instead of per adult, and bigger, poorer households look like bigger spenders for no reason but their size.

Second trap The same file doesn't even agree with itself on a calendar.

Some HCES sections report a week of spending, others a month, in the same file. Add them as-is and you're adding incompatible units — every weekly-recall item gets rescaled ×52/12 before it can sit next to a monthly one.

The layout tells you what unit each file is at. It never tells you what unit your question is at, and that's a judgment call every single time. The welfare essay compares spend per adult, deliberately, because household size correlates with income and would confound an insured-vs-uninsured comparison if I'd used household totals. Pick the wrong level to collapse at and you get a number that's arithmetically correct and substantively wrong. And there's a second, sneakier version of the same problem: HCES asks about some items over the last week and others over the last month, in the same questionnaire. Milk and vegetables are weekly recall; most of the rest is monthly. Add a weekly figure to a monthly one without rescaling and you've silently mixed two different units of time.

The weight nobody warns you about

These are large, multi-stage, stratified samples — not a simple random draw. Every row carries a survey weight, usually called multiplier (sometimes shortened to mult), recording how many real households or people that one response stands in for, nationally.

the same weighted-mean idiom, hand-rolled or reused, in every essay in this talk

def compare(df, group_cols, agg_cols, mult_col="multiplier"):
    return df.groupby(group_cols).apply(
        lambda x: (x[agg_cols] * x[mult_col].values.reshape(-1, 1)).sum(axis=0)
        / x[mult_col].sum()
    )

The mistake Skip the multiplier, and thirty survey rows can outweigh thirty million real households.

Every number in this talk is a multiplier-weighted estimate, never a raw average.

This is the single most common way a well-meaning FOSS person gets NSS data wrong: they load the file, call .mean() on a column, and get a number that looks plausible and is nowhere near the truth. Every sampled household in HCES literally carries a weight describing its importance in the overall survey, and every figure the government itself publishes is a weighted metric. This exact function lives in hces.py and gets imported by five different welfare notebooks; the workweek essay hand-rolls the identical line inline, just with a column called "mult" instead of "multiplier" — same idiom, sum of value times weight, over sum of weight. If you don't multiply by that weight, you haven't computed a national estimate — you've computed the average of whoever happened to get sampled near you.

Getting comfortable, one habit at a time

  • Download the trio together: unit-level data, the “layout” (data dictionary), and the summary report — from MoSPI's own portal, never the data alone
  • Read the layout before the data — find the unit of each file and the columns that join them
  • Apply the multiplier before you compute anything — a raw mean is not a national estimate
  • Sanity-check your topline number against the survey's own published report before trusting any deeper cut
  • Build one reusable decode-and-stitch script per survey, and publish it with the cleaned data — so future-you never redoes that translation from scratch (one hces.py, imported by five different welfare notebooks in this repo)

None of this is hard. It's just never taught — and once you've done it, “according to NSS data” stops being a hand-wave.

None of this requires a statistics degree. It's five boring habits: get the data, the layout and the report together; read the layout first; always weight; check your number against the government's own summary before you go further; and write the decoding step once, as code, so it's done forever. Do this once per survey and every headline that cites "NSS data" becomes something you can actually go check yourself.

The knack, not just the tech

Before you reach for data

  • Extend the benefit of the doubt first — Hanlon's razor
  • Then hold your own analysis to the same merciless standard you're demanding of theirs
  • If you're not sure of something, don't say it

Knowing when to stop

  • Sometimes the most honest finding is that the data cannot answer the question
  • The gig-worker case: absence of a category is itself the finding
  • A non-result is still a result — say so plainly
The tech gets you the number. The knack is a temperament: extend good faith before you open the notebook, then be just as merciless with your own numbers as you are with theirs. And sometimes — like with gig workers — the honest answer is that the question can't be answered with what we have.

Numbers alone don't move anyone

A p-value never changed anyone's mind. Carrying an insight the last mile — from analysis to impression — means pairing cold, careful numbers with a sincere, human story.

Do

  • Ground the number in a concrete, human cost — an hour of sleep, a rupee of gutkha, a tuition fee
  • Show your work: publish the data and the code

Don't

  • Stop at the abstract percentage and assume it lands
  • Mistake a confident headline for a checked one
"Women do 4x the unpaid work" is a statistic. "Men spend barely an hour a week in the kitchen, and are absent from cleanup entirely" is an image people carry out of the room. That last mile — from the number to the impression — is where the real debunking happens.

A debunking you can't reproduce is just another opinion.
