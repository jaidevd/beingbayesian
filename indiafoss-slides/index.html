Fighting misinformation isn't just hard. It's Sisyphean.

Digital transformation experts say quick commerce is killing kirana stores. Startup bros say gig work is liberating India's poor and uneducated. Captains of industry say the only way to make it is a 90-hour week. Influencers-turned-economists say welfare is making people lazy — and you just guessed at three of these yourself. Every one of these is stated with total confidence, and almost nobody checks it. Brandolini's law: the energy needed to refute bullshit is an order of magnitude bigger than the energy needed to produce it. You're pushing a boulder up a hill that resets every day.

Armed with public microdata and a Jupyter notebook, we can dismantle even the loudest stories.

As open-data and FOSS people, we're uniquely positioned to do this. India quietly publishes some of the richest microdata in the world — NSS, PLFS, HCES, the Time Use Survey — and that's only a fraction of what's out there. This talk isn't a statistics lecture. Case 1 · Retail

Quick commerce is killing kirana stores

“About 2,00,000 kirana stores have shut down in the last year because of predatory e-commerce practices.” AICPDF (traders' federation), widely reshared as breaking, shocking data

Gut check 200,000 sounds huge. Huge compared to what?

This one came from a trade federation and went everywhere on social media with SHOCKING DATA energy. Before opening a dataset: 200,000 is a big number in isolation. But India doesn't have a small number of kirana stores. Let's find out how big.

India has about 13 million kirana stores

200,000 closures against ~13M kirana stores1.5% national closure rate
… even limited to metro India alone5%
Households that shop online for groceries at all7% — 93% stay offline
… even inside urban India specifically83% still don't shop online
Historical base rate: Amazon/Flipkart-era, COVID lockdown surgedisplaced only 1–2%, then ~0.5%

HCES 2023–24

India has roughly thirteen million kirana stores. Two hundred thousand closures against that base is a 1.5% closure rate nationally, 5% if you only count metros. And only 7% of Indian households shop online for groceries at all — 93% never do. Even in urban India specifically, 83% of households don't shop online. This isn't new either: Amazon and Flipkart's big retail push only ever displaced 1 to 2% of local stores, and even the COVID lockdown e-commerce surge only got to about half a percent.

The 7% isn't evenly distributed

This chart never made it into the published piece — the district map won out because it was a cleaner, single image. But it answers who exactly the 7% is. Only 3% of Scheduled Tribe households shop online, against 27% of upper-caste ones — a nine-times gap, the starkest line in the dataset. Households without a ration card are eight times more likely to shop online than the poorest, PHH-card-holding ones. And households that had benefited from PMGKY, a COVID-era food security scheme, were four times less likely to shop online than non-beneficiaries. This essay is from March last year — the idea that welfare-scheme status predicts consumption behaviour didn't become this newsletter's whole subject until the welfare essay, ten months later. The thread was already here, in a chart that got cut.

The real rival isn't Blinkit

E-commerce can reach at most 7% of Indian households — kirana's real rival is infrastructure, education and income inequality, not quick commerce.

The posterior isn't "the closures didn't happen" — kirana stores do close, for lots of reasons. It's that the specific mechanism being blamed can, structurally, only ever touch 7% of households, and that 7% isn't randomly distributed: piped gas at home makes a household 4x more likely to be an online shopper, a postgraduate degree doubles the odds. The story blames a phone app for what's actually an infrastructure and inequality problem.
Case 2 · Labour

Gig work liberates India's poor and uneducated

“Workers who once faced barriers like lack of education or technical skills now earn ₹15,000 to ₹45,000 or even more.” the platform-economy narrative, echoing NITI Aayog's own optimistic framing

Gut check Where do we even find “gig workers” in a labour survey?

This is the LinkedIn-influencer-as-amateur-economist claim: platforms have liberated people once locked out by lack of education, and here are the numbers to prove it. Before we even reach for PLFS: does the phrase "gig worker" mean anything to India's official labour classification?

PLFS has exactly four kinds of worker

PLFS categoriesself-employed · regular wage/salaried · casual labour · not in workforce
A “gig worker” boxdoes not exist — they scatter across all four
Apply NITI Aayog's own 2022 proxy filter (urban, age 18–45, bottom 75th pctile of spend)the ₹15,000 floor holds up — the ₹45,000 ceiling doesn't
Among men in that filtered populationno meaningful income gain from more education
PLFS sorts every worker into one of four buckets, and none of them is "gig worker." So I did the next best thing: took NITI Aayog's own 2022 proxy definition — urban, 18 to 45, bottom three-quarters of per-capita spend — and applied it to the actual PLFS microdata. The 15,000-rupee floor of the claim holds up fine. The 45,000 ceiling doesn't. And within that filtered population, men see no real income bump from more education — which quietly undercuts the "liberation" framing on its own terms.

Ask for one number, get half a story

The published essay only ever reports the men's panel. The women's panel was computed — and never quoted.

This is the chart's own caption, straight from the notebook: for men, higher education pays off only by a small margin. It helps women significantly — a salaried woman with no schooling earns about 6,600 rupees a month; a salaried graduate woman earns about 14,900, more than double. But she's still earning about 24% less than a salaried male graduate, who's at about 19,600. The essay's "no meaningful income gain from education" line is true for men. It's the opposite of true for women, and that half of the finding just never got written down.

You can't check a claim about a category that doesn't exist

You can't even count India's gig workers — the surveys have no box to put them in.

This is the case where knowing when to stop matters as much as the investigation itself. I can't hand you a clean number for the gig economy's true reach or true pay, because the government's own instrument has no way to see it. That absence is the finding — and it should give anyone celebrating this liberation some pause. Sometimes the most honest, most powerful finding is that the data simply cannot answer the question.

Case 3 · Work & Care

Just work 90 hours a week

N R Narayana Murthy — 70 hrs/week · S N Subrahmanyan — 90 hrs/week · Bhavish Aggarwal — 140 hrs/week. “work–life balance is a western concept”

Gut check What would 90 hours a week actually cost — in sleep, in family time?

Three captains of industry, three escalating numbers, and the explicit dismissal of work-life balance as a foreign import. So: if a salaried, white-collar Indian actually worked 90 hours a week, what would have to give?

The hours are already unequal

Salaried, white-collar paid workmen ~56 hrs/wk · women ~48.6 hrs/wk
Unpaid domestic choreswomen do 4× as much as men
Even in this salaried cohort, men's kitchen timebarely 1 hour a week — near-absent from serving/cleanup
Kitchen time by education & marriage (women)graduates ~12.5 hrs/wk, nearly doubling below graduate level; marriage alone adds ~10 hrs/wk

2024 Time Use Survey (ICATUS)

Start with the baseline: salaried men already work about 56 hours a week, women about 48.6 — only an hour a day less, despite the perception gap. But women do four times the unpaid domestic work men do. And even in this cohort of salaried, supposedly-emancipated households, men spend barely an hour a week cooking, and are almost entirely absent from serving food or cleaning up after.

What would 90 hours actually cost?

Best case: both genders cap chores at 1 hr/daywomen's ceiling rises to only ~67 hrs/wk
To reach 90, you'd have to cut~1 hr sleep/night, all TV (10–12 hrs/wk), most talk & text time (15 hrs/wk)
Deficit still remaining after those cutswomen 22 hrs · men 34 hrs
Even in the most generous scenario — everyone caps chores at an hour a day — women's work-time ceiling only rises to 67 hours a week. Getting anyone to 90 means cutting an hour of sleep every single night, giving up television entirely, giving up most talking and texting with people you care about. And you're still 22 hours short for women, 34 for men.

Same ask, wildly different bill

A 90-hour week doesn't just cost sleep and Netflix. Because women already carry 4× the unpaid work, the same “ask” costs men and women completely different things.

This is the essay where the honest posterior isn't a single number — it's that "just work more" is not a gender-neutral instruction. The 90-hour week was proposed as a universal grind; the data says it's actually two very different asks wearing the same headline. Asking what it would cost a salaried Indian — in sleep, in family time, in the unpaid care work women already shoulder — settles the argument far better than any motivational LinkedIn post.
Case 4 · Welfare

Welfare makes people lazy — and wasteful

Poorest 40% of households spend 4% of their budget on paan/gutkha/tobacco vs. only 2.5% on education — intoxicant spend up 33% in a decade — set beside rising Ayushman Bharat (PMJAY) enrolment. Economic Advisory Council to the PM, op-ed using HCES data — the “moral hazard” framing

Gut check Are the households getting insurance the same ones spending more on gutkha?

Two of the Prime Minister's own economic advisors wrote this: government spending on health insurance is up, spending on intoxicants is up, therefore moral hazard — people take more risks because they feel insured. The hole is immediate: two things rising together doesn't mean one causes the other. Are we even looking at the same households? (The Cartman gif is the moral-hazard claim in its purest, most cartoonish form — "from this moment on, I am poor" as a strategy. It's funny because it's a two-second cartoon joke; the actual op-ed is making the same argument in a Sunday newspaper.)

Check the same households, not two trend lines

Urban PMJAY/scheme beneficiaries vs. non-beneficiariesspend ₹2/month more on gutkha
Rural beneficiaries vs. non-beneficiariesspend ₹4/month less
Entire spread across every group in the chart±₹15

HCES, propensity-score matched on household size, caste, religion & region — not a raw comparison

Instead of two national trend lines, I compared insured and uninsured households directly, split rural and urban. But not a naive comparison: households are first matched by propensity score on things like size, caste, religion and region, trimmed to common support, then compared within matched strata — the same rigor you'd want turned on any of the loud claims we started with. Urban beneficiaries spend about two rupees a month more on gutkha than urban non-beneficiaries. Rural beneficiaries actually spend four rupees less than rural non-beneficiaries. The entire chart, top to bottom, spans about fifteen rupees.

Not a scandal. A rounding error.

Free healthcare buys the average household two rupees more gutkha a month. Moral hazard, measured — and it isn't there.

This is the dreaded link between public healthcare and "wasteful" vice spending, and it comes down to a difference of about two rupees a month. That's the posterior: the claim was directionally testable, we tested it, and it evaporates.

But welfare's real effects hide where headlines don't look

LPG-subsidy households spend nearly ₹60 more on education per student — and two-thirds of that goes straight into private coaching.

While the headline claim about vice spending falls apart, a genuine welfare effect was sitting one tab over, on a subsidy nobody was even arguing about. An LPG subsidy meant for cooking gas quietly turns into a child's tuition fee. That's the kind of finding no headline thinks to go looking for.

It's not one story. It's five, pulling different ways.

LPG Subsidy → Education+₹59 total — +₹41 to coaching (published)
Health Insurance → Education−₹44 total, but +₹54 to coaching — hidden by fees falling −₹74 (never published)
Free Electricity → Education+₹74 total, but −₹70 from coaching, +₹99 to fees — the opposite direction entirely
The published essay makes the LPG finding sound like the general shape of how welfare works — a subsidy frees up cash, some of it drifts into coaching. But I ran the identical propensity-score-matched comparison on all five schemes this essay analysed, and it isn't one shape. Health insurance shows an even bigger coaching shift than LPG — fifty-four rupees, more than LPG's forty-one — except the overall education total is negative, because formal school fees dropped even more. And free electricity does the opposite of everything else: fees go up by ninety-nine rupees, coaching goes down by seventy. The essay picked the cleanest one to tell. The other two are just as real, and they complicate the story instead of completing it. Small bonus that didn't fit anywhere: midday-meal households spend 145 rupees more on ready-to-eat food at home, even after subtracting the value of meals eaten at school — the single largest food-category effect in the entire five-scheme dataset, and nobody's written a sentence about it yet.

Not the only near-miss

The bug A “good question” metric counted how often a post got referenced later — but a 2013 question has twelve years to get referenced; a 2024 one has one.

13.7% of old posts looked “good” by that measure, vs. 0.9% of new ones — a decade-long death spiral that was mostly just time, not decline.

This is from the Stack Overflow essay, a different case entirely from the four in this talk, but worth a beat because it's the same lesson learned twice. The first version of a "genuine question" metric counted how many times a question got referenced by later posts — obviously a question from 2013 has had twelve years to accumulate references, a question from 2024 has had one. That alone manufactured what looked like a steep, decade-long decline in question quality, well before ChatGPT existed. It got caught, and rebuilt as an age-fair comparison. I'm mentioning it because the welfare essay's crisis wasn't a one-off personality quirk — it's what actually happens, more than once, when you hold your own numbers to the standard you'd hold anyone else's to.

The survey isn't one file. It's dozens.

sec-1-HCQstate, district, sector of the household
sec-5.1-FDQcereals — recall: last month
sec-6.2-FDQvegetables — recall: last week
sec-10.1-CSQeducation — recall: last year
sec-12.2-CSQtobacco — recall: last week

HCQ / FDQ / CSQ / DGQ are the four actual questionnaire booklets fielded. Every section is its own file — joined back to the rest by one column: hhid.

This is what HCES actually looks like on disk — not a clean "household file, person file, item file," which isn't real. It's dozens of small tables, one per section of the actual paper questionnaire, and the recall period is baked into which section an item lives in, not into the survey as a whole — that's why cereals are asked about monthly and tobacco weekly, in the same household's file. HCQ is household characteristics, FDQ is food items, CSQ is consumables and services, DGQ is durable goods. Every single one of these tables carries a household ID column, hhid, and that's the only thing that lets you stitch them back into one household's story.

NSS doesn't sample randomly. It samples carefully.

Stage 1 — pick a village / urban block (First Stage Unit)simple random, no replacement
Stage 2 — split its households into 3 wealth tiers first, then pick 18simple random, within each tier

The wealth split — by land value in villages, car ownership in cities — exists because rich households are numerically rare. A plain random draw would barely catch any. NSS finds them on purpose.

This is straight from HCES's own methodology document. Multi-stage stratified sampling: First Stage Units are villages or urban blocks, picked by simple random sampling without replacement. Within each selected block, households get split into three second-stage strata by an affluence proxy — land possessed in rural areas, four-wheeler ownership and value in urban areas — the richest slice, a middle slice, and everyone else — and then 18 households are drawn, deliberately, from all three. That's the only reason a national estimate can say anything sensible about rich households at all: they're such a small share of the population that a naive random sample would barely include them. On top of that, the whole survey year is split into 10 rolling three-month panels so no single season dominates, and each household is visited up to three separate times to fill the different questionnaires.

Pick the wrong unit, get the wrong story

Kirana stores — collapsed to household7% of households shop online
Welfare & vice — collapsed to per adult₹2/month more, per adult — not per household
90-hour week — collapsed to person56 vs 48.6 hours, per person per week

Second trap The same file doesn't even agree with itself on a calendar.

Every file lets you collapse it to a household, a person, or an item, and each choice gives a different, equally "correct" number. Average the intoxicant spend per household instead of per adult, and bigger, poorer households look like bigger spenders for no reason but their size — the welfare essay uses per-adult on purpose, because household size correlates with income and would confound the comparison otherwise. Second, sneakier trap: some HCES sections report a week of spending, others a month, in the same survey — milk and vegetables are weekly recall, most of the rest is monthly. Add a weekly figure to a monthly one without rescaling by 52/12 and you've silently mixed two different units of time.

The weight nobody warns you about

the same weighted-mean idiom, hand-rolled or reused, in every essay in this talk

def compare(df, group_cols, agg_cols, mult_col="multiplier"):
    return df.groupby(group_cols).apply(
        lambda x: (x[agg_cols] * x[mult_col].values.reshape(-1, 1)).sum(axis=0)
        / x[mult_col].sum()
    )

The mistake Skip the multiplier, and thirty survey rows can outweigh thirty million real households.

Every row in these surveys carries a weight, usually called "multiplier," sometimes shortened to "mult" — recording how many real households or people that one response stands in for nationally. This is the single most common way a well-meaning FOSS person gets NSS data wrong: load the file, call .mean() on a column, and get a number that looks plausible and is nowhere near the truth. This exact function lives in hces.py and gets imported by five different welfare notebooks; the workweek essay hand-rolls the identical line inline. Every number in this talk is a multiplier-weighted estimate, never a raw average.

Getting comfortable, one habit at a time

  • Download data + layout + summary report together — never the data alone
  • Read the layout before the data
  • Decode only the tables your question needs
  • Apply the multiplier before you compute anything
  • Sanity-check against the survey's own published report
  • Build one reusable script per survey, publish it with the cleaned data
None of this requires a statistics degree. Download the trio together from MoSPI's own portal: unit-level data, the layout (data dictionary), and the summary report. Read the layout first — find the unit of each file and the columns that join them. Only decode the tables your actual question needs; a survey has hundreds of columns, annotating all of them is a hobby, not an analysis. Apply the multiplier before you compute anything — a raw mean is not a national estimate. Sanity-check your topline number against the survey's own published report before trusting any deeper cut. And build one reusable decode-and-stitch script per survey — in this repo, one hces.py gets imported by five different welfare notebooks. Do this once per survey and "according to NSS data" stops being a hand-wave.

Three ways an analysis goes wrong

And it's rarely the arithmetic.

Bad data — the numbers themselves are offHCES's hidden subtotal code, hiding in the cereals list
Bad knowledge — you don't know what the numbers measureNIC industry code 62, or a weekly figure mistaken for a monthly one
Bad interpretation — the numbers are fine, the reading isn't“health insurance causes vice spending” — correlation, presented as cause
This is a useful sorting bin for any claim you're skeptical of, not just these four. Bad data: something's wrong before you even open the file — HCES sneaks a subtotal into the middle of every item list, coded like any other item; forget to drop it and every food total silently doubles. Bad knowledge: the file is fine, but you don't know what its columns actually mean or measure. Bad interpretation: everything upstream was done correctly, and the conclusion is still wrong. Every claim in this talk failed on one of these three, rarely on the math. This same trap shows up outside NSS data too — a well-known Canadian survey on youth and marijuana quotes "44.8% used it in their life," "29.2% in the last year," and "11% met criteria for dependency" side by side, as if they're the same kind of number. They're not.

Even honest, correctly-weighted data can trick you

The technical name is collider bias — and it needs no bad data, no bad code, no bad intent.

Attractiveness and niceness are drawn with zero relationship to each other, by construction. But look only at people above some combined bar of "attractive enough or nice enough to date," and a strong negative correlation appears out of nowhere — because within that slice, being weaker on one trait almost requires being stronger on the other just to clear the bar. Nobody lied, nobody miscoded anything. The same caution applies to charts as much as samples — a truncated y-axis or a 3D pie chart can manufacture an effect on data that's otherwise completely honest. The lesson isn't "don't trust data," it's "ask what was excluded before you trust a correlation."

The knack, not just the tech

Before you reach for data

  • Extend the benefit of the doubt first — Hanlon's razor
  • Hold your own analysis to the same merciless standard
  • Write the investigation cold and bland — the human story comes later, on purpose

Knowing when to stop

  • Sometimes the data cannot answer the question
  • The gig-worker case: absence of a category is itself the finding
  • A non-result is still a result — say so plainly
“Look for causes, not villains. Look for systems, not heroes.”
— Hans Rosling
The tech gets you the number. The knack is a temperament: extend good faith before you open the notebook, then be just as merciless with your own numbers as you are with theirs. The reason isn't just ethics, it's tactical — the more brutal your attack on someone else's claim, the more exposed you are if you've made the same mistake. That's also why the investigation gets written cold and bland, on purpose — facts don't have feelings, and mixing in the human angle too early is how you talk yourself into believing a number before you've checked it. If you're not sure of something, don't say it. And sometimes, like with gig workers, the honest answer is that the question can't be answered with what we have. Rosling put the rest better than I can — that's the kirana story in one sentence, the point was never to put Blinkit on trial, it was to find the actual system doing the work a villain got blamed for.

Numbers alone don't move anyone

A p-value never changed anyone's mind. Carrying an insight the last mile means pairing cold, careful numbers with a sincere, human story.

The trick: tell one more story, the opposite of the one you opened with. Everyone already knows Daniel LaRusso's story. Tell Johnny Lawrence's.

Do

  • Ground the number in a concrete, human cost
  • Show your work: publish the data and the code

Don't

  • Stop at the abstract percentage and assume it lands
  • Mistake a confident headline for a checked one
"Women do 4x the unpaid work" is a statistic. "Men spend barely an hour a week in the kitchen, and are absent from cleanup entirely" is an image people carry out of the room. That last mile is where the real debunking happens. Here's a trick for it: the prior you opened with is a story everyone already knows and roots for — it's Daniel LaRusso's story, the underdog, the version everyone's heard. The posterior needs its own story, the opposite one — not more data. The story of the kid on the other side of the mat, the one nobody bothered to ask about. That's Johnny Lawrence's story. Cobra Kai worked as a show because it finally told it. Same move, every time you want a number to actually stick.
