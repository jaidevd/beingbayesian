## Whole Numbers and Half Truths

* p.93: A combination of cultural, environmental, and economic factors
  determines what’s on the table in most households; national data cannot
  capture the vast variety of food on Indian tables.

* p.95: Only about **15% of the poorest fifth of Indians are vegetarian**.

* p.97: The Hindu right continues to promote the upper-caste narrative that
  India is a vegetarian country. When E. Sreedharan said he did not even eat
  eggs and disliked others eating meat, he effectively dismissed the food
  preferences of about **98% of people in Kerala**. Similar signalling is
  sometimes associated with Nirmala Sitharaman, who often mentions avoiding
  garlic and onions.

* p.98: The Left argues that when the option to eat eggs is removed, it reflects
  creeping Brahminism that excludes religious and caste minorities; however, it
  is also misleading to portray vegetarian preferences as purely imposed by the
  BJP.

* p.99: Arya notes that if a surveyor visited her home, her mother or
  grandmother would never admit to eating meat.

* p.100: As incomes rise in India, people consume more fruits and vegetables but
  also far more dairy, fat, and sugar than recommended. Pulse consumption does
  not increase much even among the richest 5%, meat consumption still falls
  short of recommended levels, and even the richest Indians eat less protein
  than advised. Meanwhile, junk food consumption rises sharply with income.
  About **20% of rural Indians and 23% of urban Indians remain undernourished**,
  while roughly **18% fall into what the government calls the “overnourished”
  category**.

* p.101: Over time, Indians have been consuming less paan and tobacco, though
  smokers are still more likely to smoke bidis than cigarettes. Alcohol
  consumption, however, is rising. In rural areas, country liquor is most
  common, while urban drinkers prefer foreign or refined liquor or wine. Income
  strongly shapes patterns: in rural areas, toddy and country liquor consumption
  rises with class but drops among the richest 5%, while beer and refined liquor
  increase with income; in urban areas, country liquor declines as incomes rise,
  while beer and refined liquor consumption increases sharply.

* p.157: The poorest 5% of Indians spend **₹58 out of every ₹100** on food,
  while the richest 5% spend only **₹37 out of every ₹100** on food.

* p.157: For rural Indians, the largest expense remains cereals (rice or wheat
  for home consumption). Although their share has declined as incomes rise and
  diets diversify toward vegetables, milk, and meat, cereals still account for
  the biggest single share (**9.4%**) of total expenditure. In urban India,
  spending patterns have shifted: cereals stopped being the largest expense in
  **2011–12**, replaced by milk and milk products; by **2017–18**, beverages,
  refreshments, and processed foods (including eating out) became the largest
  category. The average urban Indian spends about **₹350 per month eating out**
  out of a **₹3,860 monthly budget**.

* p.160: As financial constraints ease, spending on milk rises sharply: someone
  in the **20–25th percentile in rural India spends about three times as much on
  milk** as someone in the poorest 5%. Richer households also spend more on meat
  and vegetables. Among non-food items, **health and education spending
  increases significantly with income**.

* p.250: India continues to have the **largest number of undernourished children
  in the world**, and this number **rose in 2019**, reversing decades of
  progress.


## Good Economics for Hard Times

* p.277: We will argue that this tension between cash and care should be one of
  the central concerns in the design of social policy. In the current debate, at
  one extreme, there are those who believe that the best we can do for people
  who have not flourished in the market economy is to hand them some cash and
  walk away, leaving them to find their own way in this world. At the other
  extreme stand those who have little faith in the ability of the poor to take
  care of themselves, and as a result either want to abandon them to their fate
  or to intrude heavily into their lives, restricting their choices, punishing
  those who did not fall in line. One side acts as if the self-esteem of
  beneficiaries of public programs is not an issue. The other side either does
  not care or believes it is the price they need to pay if they want public
  health. And yet, the desire to be respected is often a reason why support for
  social interventions is lacking, even among those who need them, and also a
  reason why these policies fail. In this chapter, we will explore the
  implications of this particular perspective on the design of social policies.

* p.288: In India, the government is now considering moving to what it calls
  direct benefit transfers, sending money to people's bank accounts rather than
  giving them food or other material benefits, on the grounds that it would be
  much cheaper and less subject to corruption. However, there is considerable
  opposition led mostly by left-wing intellectuals. One of them interviewed
  1,200 households throughout India about their preferences for cash versus
  food. Overall, two-thirds of the households preferred food transfers to cash.
  In states where the food distribution worked well, like in South India, this
  preference was even stronger. When asked why, 13% of households mentioned
  transaction costs, but one-third of the households who prefer food argued that
  getting foodstuff protects them against the temptation to misuse cash. In
  Dharmapuri in Tamil Nadu, one respondent said, Food is much safer, money gets
  spent easily. Another said, Even if you give me ten times the amount, I will
  prefer the ration shop, since the goods cannot be frittered away.
