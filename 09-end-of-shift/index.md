# End of Shift

## The Prior

Two years ago, I went on a boat ride on the Mekong river in Luang Prabang. There
was something ineffable about those two hours. The gorge was beautiful, wide and
flanked by sheer cliffs — some of which had caves in them. And since the gorge
was broad, the water was unnaturally still. My tour guide was skilled and
therefore, quiet. Even the boatman was skilled — he knew precisely when to shut
off the motor and let the boat be. My wife, I and the tour guide would
occasionally pass each other a banana or a beer can. But for the better part of
two hours, nobody spoke. The cliffs had an elemental effect — as if one should
fall silent in their presence. Even whipping out your phone to take a picture is
an affront to the deep, still river. It demands and obtains the toll of
singular, unflinching attention, filled with awe.

Sadly, since that trip to Laos, I have failed to curate for myself more such
experiences. It hasn't been for the lack of trying. My wife and I promised
ourselves that we would travel more, and we went to jungles, beaches, mountains
— many of them arguably more serene (if you believe travel bloggers and online
reviews — which you shouldn't) — than Luang Prabang. It is only now that I've
started developing an inkling of what the missing ingredient might be: it was,
quite simply, being present.

That trip to Laos had coincided with a major professional milestone. The hard
work of a decade had paid off handsomely, and in a very tangible fashion. The
skills acquired over fourteen years had finally amounted to something very
tangible. I decided to take a sabbatical, and so there I was, floating on the
Mekong with not a care in the world.

The sabbatical brought with it great many things — including the inception of
this very publication. But more than anything else it brought me unprecedented
clarity of thought and peace of mind. And here, I suspect, that I fell prey to
the fallacy of the single cause. The sabbatical brought me immersion, and I
thought only something like it could bring me immersion. Eventually, the
sabbatical ended and I went back to work. And with work came the questionable
distinction of being a workaholic. It had served me well in the past, and it
would serve me well now. But being a workaholic also means losing the ability to
switch off.

Back in the day my dog had learned to recognize the sound of the keyboard
falling silent. It meant that it was perhaps time for her walk. Today, that
familiar clacking is far less prominent in her life — not least because LLMs are
doing most of my typing for me; her workaholic human is now rarely available in
the evenings. She still takes him for a walk at night, but that's only because
*he* does not take *her* any more. Even late at night when they're both sitting
on a park bench, he cannot resist pulling out his phone to check the progress
his coding agents have made; but she's happy touching grass — greedily sniffing
every inch of it and rolling around in it trying to get its scent in her fur —
she doesn't know if she can bring him to the park tomorrow. She knows that his
attention is expensive. And if she had the slightest interest in anything except
sniffing grass, she'd realize that lovely as she is, she's not the Mekong.

And despite all of this evidence to the contrary, I am somehow immensely
confident in my ability to switch off. Every Friday evening I turn off
notifications from work until Monday morning. Every time I take a vacation I
uninstall Slack. And every time I fail to stay away. Most recently, on a
week-long trip to the Andaman Islands I uninstalled and reinstalled Slack twice,
and I must have checked my email at least 20 times. Thank heavens my colleagues
and I don't speak to each other on WhatsApp. That would be a nightmare. But a
part of me also knows that I'd perhaps happily live through it, too, if it
really came to that.

In short, I am Piyush Goyal's poster boy: that tireless and constantly available
Indian worker who's completely unaware of foreign ideas like the weekend or 5
o'clock — or even the sunset.

---

Last month, speaking to an audience of Dutch businessmen, Piyush Goyal said,

> We are a large country ... young, talented, skilled, aspirational, looking for
> a better quality of life for themselves, for their children, for their
> families; ... willing to work hard. Nobody in India will tell you it's 5
> o'clock, and certainly, no Indian will remind you it's the weekend.

When he said "5 o'clock" (or write this as "at '5 o'clock'") he looked at his
wristwatch. And at the end, he duly paused for laughter.

To be fair, it *is* funny in a certain sense. The hardworking, underpaid and
overworked Indian forever eager to please is far from the most offensive
stereotype. But it's also funny because the minister seems to be oblivious to
the contradiction between "looking for a better quality of life" and cheap
labour. His colleagues in Indian politics never tire of accusing each other of
having a colonial hangover, while at the same time he has no qualms advertising
the stereotype in a European country, of all places.

Not long ago he scolded Indian entrepreneurs for pretending that fast and cheap
food delivery was "innovation".[^verify] It's a delicious irony that the Union
Minister of Commerce & Industry has drawn up his full height to celebrate on
European soil what he once criticized in India.

You could argue that the speech, however, as a whole, was a good one. And you
would be right. Why, then, take these few sentences out of context? In a country
where even the PM can barely speak without a teleprompter, why make a big deal
out of an elected official not choosing their words more carefully?

I can think of at least three reasons. First, I may be a workaholic, but I'm
still jobless enough to want to die on obscure hills. Second, it is precisely
the gaffes of public figures that pave the way to illuminating reality. Third,
Piyush Goyal disliked people getting into the math of what he's said (since it
did not help Einstein discover gravity).

So that's exactly what we shall do.
