Prima facie, there's nothing wrong with Goyal's claim. It's reasonable to
believe that most Indians are still at their desk at 5 pm, and most Indian do
work on weekends. In fact, we could simply replace the generalizing phrases like
"nobody in India" with "very few people in India", and the statement could even
be taken literally. After all, even in the most emancipated work cultures, you
can barely expect people to follow the strict 9-to-5 schedule.

However, it is the _spirit_ of the statement that is under scrutiny here. What
does the working class in India do in the evenings and on weekends? Who,
precisely, are the people who work late evenings, and how do they differ from
people who don't? To anwser these questions, we turn (once again[^90hr]) to
NSO's Time Use Survey of 2024. The survey itself is comprehensive, and paints a
detailed picture of an average day in the life of practically every Indian. But
we limit ourselves only to salaried[^regularwagetoo] respodents—mostly to keep
things as relatable as possible—I'm salaried, and you are most likely salaried,
too. As expected, almost three quarters of this cohort are still working at 5
pm.[^break]

People working in education are more likely to have finished earlier, presumably
because schools and colleges have fixed schedules. Outside of education, 37% of
public sector workers and 78% of private sector workers are still working at 5
pm. So the initial claim is about half-right. By 7 pm, a quarter of salaried
employees are still working, and within two hours, only 7% are still at their
desk. Since education is a sector that drags the average, we omit it from
further analysis.

The next step is to ask what seems[^corrcause] to govern peoples' evenings—not
just the end of the day's work, but also what follows. To answer this, we divide
the non-education salaried sub-population into six mutually exclusive groups:
men and women across the three sectors of health and public administration,
knowledge services and trade, transport and hospitality. For both men and women,
work finishes earliest in the health and public administration sector, and
latest in trade / transport and hospitality.

![](assets/clock-faces.png)

Note, however, that womens' evenings are dominated by housework, which is
negligible for men. It's a bit like a gendered twist on Parkinson's law: the
earlier women finish work, the more time they spend on housework, whereas for
men, the evenings fill up with leisure.

This gender imbalance is, unfortunately, the fulcrum on which this entire piece
rests. Every story about time use, and indeed, about productivity in the labour
economy inevitably turns into a gendered problem. In the same speech, Goyal
said, "... more and more of our women are coming into the workforce; they don't
want to be washing clothes at home." He also said something about more washing
machines being sold in India than in the rest of the world put together. But
according to the HCES, only about a third of salaried households have one (46%
in cities, 21% in villages), and 21% of households overall. Nor does having one
seem to change much. In the TUS, women in households that wash clothes by
machine spend only about four minutes a day less on laundry than comparable
women in households that wash by hand, and men spend under two minutes either
way.[^controls] Households that outsource it are the exception, at about 26
minutes less, but only 2% of salaried households do. The larger point is that if
more women come into the workforce, someone still has to do the housework.

Eventually, people _will_ become more and more cognizant of sunsets and
weekends, either because more women are in the workforce, or because more men
are doing housework. Goyal's two visions of the future don't necessarily
contradict each other, but they do illuminate a tradeoff that are difficult to
reconcile.

In fact, the tradeoff becomes even more precarious when we consider the second
aspect of the Indian hustle flex: the weekend. Here, too, Goyal is technically
correct about the facts, while being spectacularly wrong about what they mean.
He's right in that many people (almost a fifth of our cohort, for an average of
six hours) work on their days off. But the burden of housework is overwhelmingly
borne by women.

These are the same women who "don't want to be washing clothes at home." Sure,
they certainly don't _want_ to. They would much rather be doing paid work. But
something tells me that the hustle flex won't survive no matter how many
dishwashers and washing machines are sold in India.


[^corrcause]: Yes, yes, I know, correlation is not causation. But throwing
    correlation away just because it is not causation is like throwing the baby
    out with the bathwater.
[^controls]: Women in salaried households, TUS 2024. The difference is 4 minutes
    a day (95% interval 3 to 6) after accounting for rural or urban, age,
    household size, per-capita spending and the woman's own work status. It is a
    comparison across households, not a measured effect of buying a machine.
    Richer households both own machines and may have more laundry. Washing
    itself is 4 minutes shorter; drying and ironing barely differ.
[^break]: "Still working" means 5 pm falls between a respondent's first and last
    half hour of paid work that day, so it includes people who are on a break at
    that moment: lunch, tea or a midday gap of up to four hours. If we count only
    those whose diary shows paid work in the 5 pm half hour itself, the figure is
    61% instead of 69%, so about 8% of salaried people are between stretches of
    paid work at 5 pm. The estimate barely moves if the break limit is anywhere
    from one to six hours (67% to 69%). Another 27% have finished for the day
    and 1% did no paid work that day. The remaining 3% are mostly people whose
    gap in paid work at 5 pm is longer than four hours. Of those who did any
    paid work that day, about 73% have not yet finished.
