**Why maternity benefits?**

(1) The thinking behind maternity benefits is that the process of raising children should also be recognized as work.

> **Note:** Look at care work economics in the Time Use Survey and other sources.

(2) Free health services during pregnancy and immediately after childbirth, and (3) job security during the leave period. In the unorganized sector, India's position is close to the lowest in the world and far below the ILO's recommendation, while in the organized sector it exceeds the ILO's recommendation.

Second, few women in Indian society are economically self-reliant, which limits their capacity to support themselves. Those who are employed, in the absence of maternity benefits, are forced to choose between family and career: when they become pregnant and cannot get leave from their jobs, they have to quit, and by the time their children have grown up, they face difficulties re-entering the job market. If maternity benefits were their entitlement, they could do both, holding a job and raising children, at the same time.

## Page 32

Third, women have certain special nutritional and health needs during pregnancy. Data show that malnutrition and physical weakness are more prevalent among Indian women. According to the NFHS, in 2019–21, 57 percent of women aged 15 to 49 had a deficiency of blood, that is, anemia. What is worrying is that before that, in NFHS 2015–16, the figure was 53 percent, so their condition has not improved. According to the NFHS, one in every five women has a very low weight for her height. Medical research tells us that a mother's health also affects the health of her child.

Fourth, maternity benefits give women a chance to rest without giving up their earnings and economic independence. Otherwise, they can either rest, or use their earnings to meet their nutritional and health needs, but not both.

Fifth, for a child's health, exclusive breastfeeding is needed for six months. At this age, children have to be fed at short intervals. If a mother is to breastfeed her child, she will need leave from work. Women who receive maternity benefits have access to this facility.

There are two main streams of research on these issues: one on the impact of maternity benefits in developed countries, and the other on their effects in developing countries. In developed countries, studies of maternity benefits focus on three main outcomes: fertility rate, child mortality rate, and women's work participation rate. In developing countries, maternity benefits may additionally affect breastfeeding and the child's weight at birth. Such studies are more numerous in developed countries, because in developing countries, where the unorganized sector is large, maternity benefits are not very widespread. **Midday Meals and Education**

The midday meal is linked to children's right to education. Children studying in government schools come from fairly poor households. Many are sent to school on an empty stomach in the morning and eat only after returning home from school. Can a child whose stomach is growling with hunger possibly concentrate on studies?

## Page 70

A discussion of the midday meal scheme would be incomplete without talking about women. The scheme holds special importance for women. For working mothers, not having to send food to school with their children makes the midday meal a big support. On the other hand, almost all cooks and helpers in the midday meal scheme are women. Looking at Table A4 (see the statistical appendix), Bihar (59 percent) and Gujarat (69 percent) are the only two states where women's share of the cook and helper category is below 80 percent. It is notable that, contrary to the worldwide trend, the proportion of working women in India is declining over time rather than rising. In such circumstances, the midday meal scheme provides employment to 20 lakh women across the country. Surprisingly, questions are still being raised in the country about the usefulness of this scheme. In many developed and developing countries around the world, midday meals are provided free to all school children; these include countries such as Finland and Brazil. Then there are many countries that have midday meals but where parents also bear some of the cost depending on their economic status; this category includes countries such as the USA, England, and France. In India, the Food Security Act passed in 2013 also covers the school midday meal scheme. India is not the only country with such a law. For example, Japan introduced a School Meal Law in 1954, and in Finland it is included in the 1948 education law.

**Policy Focus Strays from the Real Issues**

Over the past ten years, India's midday meal scheme could have become comparable to a similar scheme in a developed country like Japan, had it received political and administrative blessing. But to some extent, the central government lost focus. The central government's spending on the midday meal scheme as a percentage of GDP was highest in 2007–08 and 2008–09, when it was 0.15 percent of GDP. Today, it has fallen to a mere 0.03 percent of GDP. It is true that enrolment in government schools has declined to some extent, especially in states where population growth has slowed, and partly also because enrolment in private schools has increased. But even today, under the central government's norms, only ₹6 per day is spent on each primary school child and ₹8 on each upper primary child. Some states, such as Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and Telangana, contribute somewhat more on their own, so in these states ₹8 to ₹9.50 is spent per child per day. The wages of the women who cook meals in schools are also quite low, in some states even below the government minimum wage rate. Leaving all these issues aside, policymakers' attention has strayed to inappropriate technology and centralized kitchens.

Government intervention was necessary because, if it had been left to the market, increased production would have caused market prices to fall, and farmers would have suffered losses from the Green Revolution instead of benefiting. In the 1960s, the Food Corporation of India began buying wheat and rice at minimum support prices. Grain storage has three main objectives: (1) controlling grain prices in the country: if there is inflation, the government tries to bring prices down by selling the Food Corporation's grain in the market; (2) a buffer stock, that is, for use in disasters such as famine or war; and (3) distribution through the PDS for food security. As productivity and output grew, procurement and storage also increased. On the other hand, pressure also built for expanding the PDS.

The National Food Security Act (NFSA) was passed in 2013. The law has four main schemes: the Public Distribution System, the midday meal, the Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS), and the Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana for maternity benefits. Through it, 10 to 12 crore children receive midday meals, and nutrition and health services are provided to anganwadi children. Together with the maternity benefit provision, the cost of all this is roughly 2 percent of the country's GDP.

The law brought three major changes to the Public Distribution System. First, the proportion of ration card holders in the country's population increased. Under the Food Security Act, two-thirds of the country's population was brought under the PDS. The Act gave 75 percent of people in rural areas and 50 percent in cities the right to receive cheap grain through ration cards. Since the Food Security Act came into force, 80 crore people are covered by the PDS. Along with this, an attempt was made to abolish the unreliable BPL category, which succeeded only to some extent.

The second major change concerned the prices of wheat and rice. For example, before the law, the central government was supplying grain to states for BPL households at ₹4.65 per kg. Under the Food Security Act, priority households receive grain at ₹1 per kg for coarse grains, ₹2 for wheat, and ₹3 for rice.

The third change was that members of priority households now receive 5 kg of grain per person per month, and Antyodaya households receive 35 kg per month. Earlier, each BPL household received 20–35 kg of grain depending on the state. The law formally abolished the BPL category, although even today priority households are still called BPL in many states, and in some states the BPL category is still in use.
