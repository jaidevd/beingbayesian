**The Difference Between NPS and OPS**

The NPS is a defined-contribution scheme: every month, the employee and their employer put a portion of the salary (10 to 15 percent) into a pension fund. The money deposited in this pension fund is invested in various places, such as mutual funds and bonds, and when you retire, you will receive monthly the returns earned on these investments, depending on the state of the stock market. The NPS has problems. The United States has a similar pension system, and during the 2007–08 financial crisis, the pension fund decision-makers sank the pensions of thousands of teachers. The biggest flaw of the NPS is risk. The remedy is to keep investments safe and to protect the returns from them against risk. For this, it is essential to monitor those who control the funds and to bring transparency into investment decisions. Making regulatory bodies transparent and keeping them in check is easy to say, but the American experience shows it is very hard to do.

The OPS had a defined benefit: when you retire, you receive 50 percent of the salary from your last post as your pension. It also includes protection against inflation. Where will the money for this come from? This pension liability is met from revenue receipts. According to one estimate, at the state level, spending on pensioners amounts to one-quarter of tax revenue.

**Questions Raised by the OPS**

The pensions of employees earning anywhere from a few thousand to lakhs of rupees per month are paid out of direct and indirect taxes collected from the people of the country, to which even the poorest of the poor contribute. Seen this way, OPS pensioners also depend on taxes paid by the poor. Is it fair that the pensions of the country's most affluent class are paid out of tax money collected from the poorest class?

**Social Security Pensions: A Solution**

When health services depend on private insurance, a different kind of problem arises. Those who buy insurance are more likely to fall ill; otherwise, why would anyone spend money on premiums? **Why Was Demonetization Needed?**

The government's claim that there is a great deal of black money in the country was correct, but its remedy for eliminating it, demonetization, was wrong. In the case of UPA benefits, those entitled under these laws receive benefits year after year, month after month, or daily. Here too some self-targeting is possible, in the sense that only those who cannot find any other work turn up for NREGA work, and the children studying in government schools and anganwadis come from poor families. Unlike the NDA schemes, which carry no legal entitlement, the UPA schemes are enshrined in law, so it is not easy to ignore them entirely.

## Page 212

Despite legal compulsions, UPA schemes, especially those for children, faced budgetary neglect under the NDA. The midday meal and ICDS suffered heavy cuts: compared with 2014–15, spending on them fell by 40 percent in real terms by 2023. The share of UPA schemes in the central budget gradually declined from about 1.5 percent of GDP in 2014 to 1.1 percent of GDP in 2019–20, when COVID forced the government to increase it. Did NDA schemes make up for the reduced spending on UPA schemes? Barely. Although spending on NDA schemes rose steadily over the past decade, it remained below 1 percent of GDP. So it is no surprise that these schemes' achievements have been very modest. According to NFHS-5, LPG use rose from 43 percent in 2015–16 to 58 percent in 2019–21. Over this period, the proportion of households practising open defecation halved, and the proportion of households living in pucca houses rose from 56 percent to 60 percent. The reality is that the law made the current PDS coverage of 80 crore people possible. Yet the scheme is now associated far more with the BJP and Narendra Modi than with the UPA or the Congress. This is remarkable, because the NDA is earning the credit despite providing very little.

Some have argued that as GDP grows, even 1–2 percent of GDP is a much larger sum today than it was 20 years ago. According to the same observers, given that per capita GDP has also risen, there is nothing wrong if welfare spending stays constant as a percentage of GDP. This argument is wrong in at least three ways. First, it assumes that when GDP grows, everyone gets an equal share of the bigger pie. We know this is not true in India; inequality in the country has increased. Second, the past decade has seen major economic disasters: demonetization, the haphazard implementation of GST, and the humanitarian crisis caused by the COVID-19 lockdown. All three called for greater social support. Finally, as an economy grows, we expect more generous welfare spending, not less.
